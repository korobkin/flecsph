Our ultimate interest is obtaining GW waveform from BNS mergers using this method.
For example, consider two orbiting bodies, such as neutron stars, of masses $m_1$
and $m_2$. Though neutron stars are extended bodies, we assume
  they are point masses for the time being. In the center-of-mass
frame of the bodies, we can can write the equations of motion as
\cite{Goldstein}:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gw:eom:com}
  \mu\ddot{\myvec{r}} = -\myvec{\nabla} U_{\text{eff}}(\myvec{r}),
\end{equation}
where $\myvec{r}$ is the separation vector, 
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:def:com}
  \mu := \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2},
\end{equation}
is the reduced mass, and 
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:def:ueff}
  U_{\text{eff}}(\myvec{r}r) = - \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r} + \frac{L_z^2(\myvec{r})}{2\mu r^2},
\end{equation}
is the effective potential energy for some angular momentum vector
$$\myvec{L}=\myvec{r}\times m \myvec{v},$$
which we have chosen to align purely along the
$\hat{z}$-axis of our coordinate system.

For large radii, the gravitational attraction dominates Eqn~\ref{eq:def:ueff}. 
However, for small radii, the repulsive
angular momentum term dominates. This effect is called the
angular momentum barrier. It implies that, at least in
Newtonian gravity, binary systems do not merge.

Of course, neutron stars are general relativistic systems and in
general relativity, binary systems \textit{do} merge. In the
relativistic case, angular momentum is carried away via gravitational
waves and the angular momentum barrier is reduced. 

Because our code is Newtonian, in order to study binary mergers, 
we must account for dissipation of angular momentum via gravitational waves. We
incorporate this gravitational wave radiation reaction via
post-Newtonian (PN) theory. 
To implement PN theory, we use a similar
technique in~\cite{ZhugeCoalesceGW} which is based on
~\cite{FinnEvansDetermining,CentrellaMcMillanGW} 
that develop a
framework for extracting gravitational wave signatures from SPH-type
simulations. For a detailed review of post-Newtonian theory in the
context of compact binary coalescence, see
\cite{blanchet2006gravitational}.

\subsection{Compute Acceleration}

The relativistic 2-body problem can be solved with numerical relativity, or by approximate analytical methods such as the PN expansion or via a perturbation-based self-force approach ($\dfrac{m_1}{m_2}\ll1$).
The former is a GR approximation, valid for weak gravitational field and slow matter motion, so it applies to compact object binary mergers with low mutual gravity, and breaks down when the stars get too close together.

The nth order PN approximation corresponds to $\left(\dfrac{v}{c}\right)^{2n}$ or $\left(\dfrac{G m}{r c^2}\right)^n$, often abbreviated as $c^{-2n}$, with the zeroth order corresponding to Newtonian gravity.

The NS of radius $R_1$ with respect to the center of mass (that we take to be at the zero coordinate) has an acceleration in polar coordinates
$$a_1=\begin{bmatrix}\ddot R_1-R_1\Omega^2\\2\dot R_1\Omega+R_1\dot\Omega\end{bmatrix}_{r,\theta} , $$ 
with the Kepler 3rd law generalized as
$$\Omega=\sqrt{\dfrac{Gm}{r^3}}\left[1-\dfrac{Gm}{rc^2}\dfrac{3-\eta}{2}+\left(\dfrac{Gm}{rc^2}\right)^2\dfrac{15+47\eta+3\eta^2}{8}\right],$$
plus terms of order $\Ord{c^{-6}}$. The orbital frequency increase is given 
by 
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pn:omegadot}
  \dot\Omega=\dfrac{96}{5}\dfrac{Gm\eta}{r^3}\left(\dfrac{Gm}{rc^2}\right)^{5/2}+\mathcal O(c^{-6}),
\end{equation}
while the secular decrease in the orbital separation becomes  
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pn:rdot}
  \dot R_1=-\dfrac{64}{5}\dfrac{G^3m^2m_2\eta}{r^3c^5}+\mathcal O(c^{-6}),
\end{equation}
These are the first PN terms due to GW radiation reaction, taken from \cite{Blanchet2014} at 2.5PN order, with $r$ in harmonic coordinates. Note that $\ddot R$ is zero at this order.

We use the following definitions:  the total mass is $m=m_1+m_2$, the dimensionless reduced mass is $\eta=\dfrac{m_1m_2}{m^2}=\dfrac{\mu}{m}$, and the orbital separation is $r=R_1+R_2$ and is calculated by finding the center of mass position of each star.

Then we rest the Keplerian terms from the acceleration:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gw:polar:acceleration}
  a_1=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{G^2mm_2}{c^2r^3}\left(3-\eta\right)-\dfrac{G^3m^2m_2}{c^4r^4}\left(6+\dfrac{41}{4}\eta+\eta^2\right)\\-\dfrac{32}{5}\dfrac{G^{7/2}m^{5/2}m_2\eta}{c^5r^{9/2}}\end{bmatrix}_{r,\theta} ,
\end{equation}
plus terms of order 6. Map it to the set of SPH particles constituting the given NS as $a_{1,r}^{\rm part}=a_{1,r}$, $a_{1,\theta}^{\rm part}=\dfrac{r}{R_1}a_{1,\theta}$,  and add it to the momentum SPH equation after passing it to cartesian coordinates through the rotation endomorphism
$$\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.$$
We input initial velocities including Keplerian velocities to the initial model
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:initial:velocities:matrix}
  v_1=\begin{bmatrix}\dot R_1\\R_1\Omega\end{bmatrix}_{r,\theta}\text{, with }R_1=\dfrac{m_2}{m}r ,
\end{equation}
Another option is to take the acceleration from the computed positions and velocities of each star center of mass
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gw:vr:acceleration}
  \vec a_i=-\Omega^2\vec R_i-\dfrac{32}{5}\dfrac{(Gm)^3\eta}{c^5r^4}\vec v_i+\Ord{c^{-6}},
\end{equation}
with $\Omega^2$ given without the Keplerian contribution, plus terms of order 6
$$\Omega^2=\dfrac{Gm}{r^3}\left[-\dfrac{Gm}{rc^2}\left(3-\eta\right)+\left(\dfrac{Gm}{rc^2}\right)^2\left(6+\dfrac{41}{4}\eta+\eta^2\right)\right].$$

\section{Extract Gravitational Waveforms}

In \texttt{FleCSPH}, we calculate gravitational wave radiation using the quadrupole approximation, 
which is valid for nearly Newtonian approximation (MTW Gravitation, 1972). The spacetime metric is
\begin{align}
g_{ab} = \eta_{ab} + h_{ab}
\end{align}
where $a,b=0,1,2,3$, $\eta_{ab} = diag(-1,1,1,1)$ is the usual Minkowski spacetime, and $|h_{ab}| \ll 1$.
Without detailed discussion (since this is fairly standard for GR courses, you can find many references),
the gravitational waveforms can be obtained by transverse-traceless (TT) components of $h_{ij}$
\begin{align}
\label{eqn:met:tt}
h^{TT}_{ij} = \frac{G}{c^4} \frac{2}{r} \ddot{Q}^{TT}_{ij}
\end{align}
where $G$ is a gravitational constant and $c$ is the speed of light. 
$Q_{ij}$ is the reduced (i.e. traceless) quadrupole moment of the source such that
\begin{align}
Q_{ij} = \int \rho \left(x_i x_j - \frac{1}{3} r^2 \delta_{ij} \right) d^3 r 
\end{align}
where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta, a dot indicates a time derivative $d/dt$. 
Here spatial indices $i,j = 1,2,3$ and  $r^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$.

To calculate $Q_{ij}$ for the distribution of particles, we use the usual fundamental
SPH equation for density. Since the gravitational radiation quantities do not feed
back into the dynamical equations, there is no need to symmetrize them in smoothing length.
Using this argument, we can rewrite $Q_{ij}$
\begin{align}
\label{eqn:quad:int:SPH}
Q_{ij} = \sum_p m_p \int W(|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_p|, h_p) \left(x_i x_j - \frac{1}{3} r^2 \delta_{ij}\right) d^3 r 
\end{align}
Here we assume that a SPH kernel is normalized. For example, consider 
$W(|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_p|, h_p) = \delta(|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_p|)$, which is the point mass limit. 
Then we immediately obtain 
\begin{align}
Q_{ij} = \sum_p m_p \left(x_{ip} x_{jp} - \frac{1}{3} \delta_{ij} r^2_p \right)
\end{align}
where $x_{ip}$ is the $x_i$ coordinate of particle $p$. We can extend this for any
normalized, spherically symmetric kernels (which are our choices of kernels in \texttt{FleCSPH}).

Consider $xx$ component first, then we have
\begin{align}
Q_{xx} = \sum_p m_p \int W(|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_p|, h_p) \left(x^2 - \frac{1}{3} r^2 \right) d^3 r 
\end{align}
Making the change of variables, $R = r-r_p$ and so $X=x-x_p$, $Y=y-y_p$, and $Z=z-z_p$ 
gives
\begin{align}
Q_{xx} &= \sum_p m_p \int W(\vec{R},h_p) \left[ (X+x_p)^2 - \frac{1}{3} 
                \left\{ (X+x_p)^2 + (Y+y_p)^2 + (Z+z_p)^2 \right\} \right] d^3 R \nonumber \\
            &= \sum_p m_p \int W(\vec{R},h_p) \left[ X^2 - \frac{1}{3}R^2 + \frac{4}{3} x_p X 
            -\frac{2}{3} y_p Y -\frac{2}{3} z_p Z + \left(x_p^2 - \frac{1}{3} r_p^2\right) \right] d^3 R 
\end{align}
Now we perform usual coordinate transformation for spherical coordinate i.e. $X = R \sin \Theta \cos \Phi$,
$Y = R \sin \Theta \sin \Phi$, and $Z = R \cos \Theta$. Then $Q_{xx}$ becomes
\begin{align}
Q_{xx} =& \sum_p \int W(\vec{R}, h_p) \bigg[ R^2 \sin^2 \Theta \cos^2 \Phi - \frac{1}{3} R^2 
                +\frac{4}{3} x_p R \sin \Theta \cos \Phi \nonumber \\
               &\indent - \frac{2}{3} y_p R \sin \Theta \sin \Phi 
                -\frac{2}{3} z_p R \cos \Theta + \left( x_p^2 - \frac{1}{3} r_p^2\right) \bigg] d^3 R \nonumber \\
             = & \sum_p \int_0^{h_p} \int_0^\pi \int_0^{2\pi} W(\vec{R}, h_p) \bigg[ R^2 \sin^2 \Theta \underbrace{ \cos^2 \Phi}_{=\pi \,\, \textrm{for}\,\,  \Phi \,\, \textrm{int}} - \frac{1}{3} R^2 
                +\frac{4}{3} x_p R \sin \Theta  \underbrace{\cos \Phi}_{=0 \,\, \textrm{for}\,\,  \Phi \,\, \textrm{int}}\nonumber \\
               &\indent - \frac{2}{3} y_p R \sin \Theta \underbrace{\sin \Phi}_{=0 \,\, \textrm{for}\,\,  \Phi \,\, \textrm{int}} 
                -\frac{2}{3} z_p R \cos \Theta + \left( x_p^2 - \frac{1}{3} r_p^2\right) \bigg] R^2 \sin \Theta\, d \Phi d\Theta dR \nonumber \\
                =& \sum_p \int_0^{h_p} \int_0^\pi \int_0^{2\pi} W(\vec{R}, h_p) 
                \left( x_p^2 - \frac{1}{3} r_p^2\right) R^2 \sin \Theta\, d \Phi d\Theta dR \nonumber \\
              &+   \sum_p \int_0^{h_p} \int_0^\pi W(\vec{R}, h_p) \left[ \pi R^4 \underbrace{\sin^3}_{=4/3 \,\, \textrm{for}\,\,  \Phi \,\, \textrm{int}} \Theta - \frac{2\pi}{3} R^4 \underbrace{\sin\Theta}_{=2 \,\, \textrm{for}\,\,  \Phi \,\, \textrm{int}} 
              - \frac{2}{3} z_p R \underbrace{\cos \Theta \sin \Theta}_{=0 \,\, \textrm{for}\,\,  \Theta\,\, \textrm{int}} \right] d\Theta dR \nonumber \\
                =& \sum_p \int_0^{h_p} \int_0^\pi \int_0^{2\pi} W(\vec{R}, h_p) 
                \left( x_p^2 - \frac{1}{3} r_p^2\right) R^2 \sin \Theta\, d \Phi d\Theta dR \nonumber \\ 
                &+  \sum_p \int_0^{h_p}  W(\vec{R}, h_p) \left(\frac{4 \pi}{3} R^4 - \frac{4 \pi}{3} R^4\right) \nonumber \\
                =& \sum_p  \left( x_p^2 - \frac{1}{3} r_p^2\right)\underbrace{ \int_0^{h_p} \int_0^\pi \int_0^{2\pi} W(\vec{R}, h_p) 
                R^2 \sin \Theta\, d \Phi d\Theta dR}_{=1} \nonumber \\ 
                =& \sum_p  \left( x_p^2 - \frac{1}{3} r_p^2\right)
\end{align}
The remaining components are evaluated similarly. WLOG, we can conclude
\begin{align}
Q_{ij} = \sum_p m_p \left(x_{ip} x_{jp} - \frac{1}{3} \delta_{ij} r_p^2 \right)
\end{align}
for any normalized spherically symmetric kernels. Now we need to evaluate time derivative of $Q_{ij}$.
Since we are using SPH, time derivative of $Q_{ij}$ can be evaluated directly by using particles' positions,
velocities and accelerations. 

Let's consider $Q_{xx}$ again. We can explicitly rewrite $Q_{xx}$ in terms of Cartesian component such that
\begin{align}
Q_{xx} = \frac{1}{3} \sum_p m_p (2x_p^2 - y_p^2 - z_p^2) 
\end{align}
Taking time derivative gives
\begin{align}
\dot{Q}_{xx} = \frac{2}{3} \sum_p m_p (2 x_p v_{xp} - y_p v_{yp} - z_p v_{zp})
\end{align}
where $v_{xp}$ is a velocity in $x$ of particle $p$ (same for $y$, and $z$). Take one more time derivative, then we have
\begin{align}
\ddot{Q}_{yy} = \frac{2}{3} \sum_p m_p (2 x_p a_{xp} - y_p a_{yp} - z_p a_{zp} + 2 v^2_{xp} - v^2_{yp} - v^2_{zp})
\end{align}
where $a_{xp}$ is an acceleration in $x$of particle $p$ Other diagonal components are can be obtained in same way
\begin{align}
\ddot{Q}_{yy} = \frac{2}{3} \sum_p m_p (2 x_p a_{xp} - y_p a_{yp} - z_p a_{zp} + 2 v^2_{xp} - v^2_{yp} - v^2_{zp}) \\
\ddot{Q}_{zz} = \frac{2}{3} \sum_p m_p (2 x_p a_{xp} - y_p a_{yp} - z_p a_{zp} + 2 v^2_{xp} - v^2_{yp} - v^2_{zp})
\end{align}
For off-diagonal components, let's consider $Q_{xy}$
\begin{align}
Q_{xy} = \sum_p m_p x_p y_p
\end{align}
Taking successive time derivative gives
\begin{align}
\dot{Q}_{xy} &= \sum_p m_p (x_p v_{yp} + y_p v_{xp})\\
\ddot{Q}_{xy} &= \sum_p m_p ( x_p a_{yp} + y_p a_{xp} + 2 v_{xp} v_{yp})
\end{align}
Remaining independent off-diagonal terms are
\begin{align}
\ddot{Q}_{xz} &= \sum_p m_p ( x_p a_{zp} + z_p a_{xp} + 2 v_{xp} v_{zp})\\
\ddot{Q}_{yz} &= \sum_p m_p ( y_p a_{yp} + z_p a_{xp} + 2 v_{yp} v_{zp})
\end{align}
Since $Q_{ij}$ is symmetric, remaining off-diagonal terms are defined
\begin{align}
Q_{yx} = Q_{xy}, \,\,\,\, Q_{zx} = Q_{xz}, \,\,\,\, Q_{zy} = Q_{yz}
\end{align}
Same for all time derivative terms.

The gravitational waveforms are given by the TT components of the metric perturbation
in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:met:tt}. In general, this term depends on the direction in which the radiation
is emitted relative observers. Thus, we need to choose coordinate basis for decomposing

e_\theta &= \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \\
e_\varphi &= \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \varphi}
\end{align}
